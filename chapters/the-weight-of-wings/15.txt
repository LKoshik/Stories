Her room was small.

A bed, a rack for clothes along one wall, a window directly behind the headboard that had been locked against the sun. It didn't stop the light entirely — nothing short of a wall could have — but it reduced it to something manageable, a dim filtered version of the morning that made the room feel like it existed slightly outside of time.

Her mother sat beside her on the bed.

Sia drank the water her mother had brought — tilted the glass back, took too much at once, and sat there for a moment with her cheeks blown out, holding it, buying herself a second.

She swallowed. Returned the glass.

Her mother was looking at her in the particular way that mothers looked when they were deciding whether to say something.

Sia felt it coming.

"Is everything—"

"I brought a dress for the function," Sia said. "Do you want to see it?"

Her mother looked at her.

"For a function?"

"It's a nice dress."

"Sia."

That was it. Just her name, in that specific register that meant the conversation was over and had been over before it started. Sia's face did something that wasn't quite a pout and wasn't quite acceptance but landed somewhere between the two.

"Get freshened up," her mother said, already standing. "I have a saree. I kept it for exactly this kind of occasion."

Sia nodded and went to the bathroom.

She turned on the tap.

And then, with the water running and the door closed and nobody on the other side who could see her face, she cried. Not the long kind — just the release kind, the kind that had been waiting since the train and had been patient through the station and the auto and the stomach joke and the drive home and had finally found a door it could slip through.

It didn't take long.

She washed her face. Checked her eyes in the small mirror above the tap. Took a breath.

Turned off the water.

Came out.

— — —

"Wear this."

Green silk. Gold border. The kind of saree that had opinions about the occasion and expected to be taken seriously. Her mother held it with the confidence of someone who had already decided and was simply informing the relevant parties.

Sia looked at it.

Then she looked at her mother.

Then she turned slightly and held out her arms in the particular posture of someone submitting to a process they have no intention of fighting.

Her mother draped it. Efficiently, without wasted movement, with strong opinions about the correct tension of each fold and the precise placement of the pallu. Sia stood and was arranged — turned when indicated, arms raised and lowered on instruction, the whole thing managed with the calm competence of someone who had been doing this for decades and found it no more complicated than folding laundry.

When it was done her mother stepped back and looked at her.

Something in her expression shifted — not dramatically, just a small softening around the eyes. The particular look of a mother who has put her daughter in something and found the result satisfying in a way she wasn't going to make a production of.

"Come," she said, and turned toward the door.

Sia followed. Green silk, gold border, grandmother's watch on her wrist.

— — —

The car belonged to a neighbour who had been recruited for the morning's logistics without, as far as Sia could tell, any particular reluctance. Seven people. One car. A journey of twenty minutes that began immediately with two people in the front seats arguing pleasantly about which route was faster, both of them certain, neither of them unpleasant about it, the argument going in gentle circles the way these arguments always did.

A child in someone's lap. Sia wedged between her mother and a cousin she hadn't seen in two years.

The cousin — Deepa, three years older, recently married — looked at her with genuine warmth.

"You look good."

"Thank you." A beat. "You too."

Deepa smiled.

Sia smiled back.

The car found its route, one of the two options winning by default, and moved through the familiar roads toward the function.

— — —

The house announced itself before they arrived.

Sound first — the specific layered noise of a gathering that had been going for a while and had found its rhythm. Children running on surfaces not designed for running. The puja bells. Somewhere inside, cooking that already knew it was going to be complimented and was simply waiting for the opportunity.

And voices — the particular sound of people discussing their influence and achievements and the achievements of their children, laughing in the way of people who are comfortable and want to be seen being comfortable.

Sia took a breath as they got out of the car.

She exhaled slowly.

She positioned herself slightly behind her parents as they moved toward the entrance and performed an immediate tactical assessment — the layout, the people, the corners, the spots where the crowd thinned enough to offer temporary cover without looking conspicuous.

She had a mission. It was simple. Tick her attendance with the main people, exchange the necessary pleasantries, locate the food, find a corner, finish eating, survive.

She could do this.

— — —

She was, by all external measures, dazzling.

The green saree helped. The gold border caught the light in the way gold borders were designed to catch light and people noticed and said so and she received each compliment with a warm smile and a slight deflection that made her seem appropriately humble, which generated further compliments, which required further receiving.

It was exhausting in the specific way that performing effortlessness was always exhausting.

She moved through the function with the practiced ease of someone who had been doing this long enough to know the choreography. Her aunt — the appropriate warmth, the right questions asked and answered. Her uncle — slightly more formal, slightly more evaluating, the pleasantries exchanged with the efficiency of a transaction both parties wanted completed.

Others approached in intervals. Faces she was supposed to know, names she was supposed to have retained from the last function and the one before that. She received each one with a smile that said of course I remember you while her brain ran a completely separate and increasingly desperate operation in the background — cross referencing faces against a mental database that was frankly underfunded for the scale of this family.

Some she placed. Some she didn't.

For the ones she didn't she smiled warmly and asked about them instead — deflecting with interest, which people always appreciated and which bought her enough time to either figure it out or escape before being asked anything that required knowing who they were. It was a technique she had developed over years of exactly this situation and it had never once failed her.

She received three compliments on the saree that she was fairly certain came from people she had never met in her life.

She thanked all three of them genuinely.

And then, with the main obligations discharged, she began moving toward the food.

Not quickly enough to look purposeful. Not slowly enough to be intercepted.

She took a plate. Served herself a modest amount — enough to not attract commentary, not enough to require sitting for long. She moved with the instinct of someone navigating a space they have mapped in advance, slipping away from her parents by degrees, finding the current of the crowd and using it until she arrived, naturally and without drama, at a corner that was populated enough to not look like hiding and unrecognized enough to not require conversation.

A chair appeared — someone had just left it, the timing precise enough to feel like a small mercy from the universe.

She sat.

She ate quickly. The food was good — she registered this distantly, the way you registered things when your primary attention was elsewhere. She kept her eyes moving, her posture relaxed, her expression that of someone perfectly content with their corner and their plate and their situation.

The plate was almost empty.

Almost there.

"Sia."

A voice. Familiar. Coming from her left.
