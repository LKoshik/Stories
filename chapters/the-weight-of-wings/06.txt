She had expected today to feel different.

And it did — just not in the way she'd anticipated. The nervousness she'd carried on the train, the rehearsed casualness she'd prepared for facing him after last night, the careful plan to act like a person who had not waved through a train window like an overexcited child — all of it evaporated the moment she walked through the office doors.

Because the office was already chaos.

Not the dramatic kind — no alarms, no shouting. Just that particular low-frequency urgency that fills a workspace when something has gone wrong and everyone knows it and nobody has time to explain it to you. People moved with purpose, passing printed sheets across desks, talking in tight clusters that dissolved and reformed. Keyboards were being used like they owed someone money.

Sia drifted through it all unnoticed.

She moved through the floor quietly, past people who looked through her rather than at her, and made her way to her desk with the particular invisibility of someone who has not yet earned a place in a crisis.

She set her bag down slowly, pulled out her chair, and looked at the desk beside hers.

Veer was typing like the fate of something important depended on the next thirty seconds.

She sat carefully, trying not to exist too loudly.

He finished whatever he was doing — a final sharp keystroke, a brief scan of the screen — and turned to her.

"Good morning, Sia."

Like that. Completely level. The office dissolving around him into background noise.

She felt something loosen slightly in her chest. "Good — good morning."

"So." He turned his monitor slightly so she could see. "We have a situation."

— — —

The problem, as he explained it, belonged to their reporting pipeline — a system that pulled financial data from three sources, processed it, and delivered clean summaries to a mid-sized client every week. Somewhere in the last update, a mapping error had crept in silently. Not a crash. Not an obvious failure. Just numbers that were slightly, consistently, invisibly wrong.

The client had a review on Monday.

Today was Friday.

"The error is somewhere in the transformation layer," Veer said, pulling up the relevant files. "We need to find it, fix it, revalidate the last two weeks of reports, and reformat the output before end of day. It's not a one person job which is why you're on it with me."

Sia looked at the screen. Then at him. "What's my part?"

He showed her — the validation side, cross-referencing the corrected output against the original source data, flagging discrepancies, documenting changes. Detail work. Careful work. Not impossible, but not nothing either.

She thought about her second day. About the word prove.

"I can handle it," she said.

He nodded once and turned back to his screen.

— — —

She could not, as it turned out, entirely handle it.

Not at first. The validation logic was layered in a way the onboarding document had not prepared her for, and every time she thought she understood the structure something underneath it behaved differently than expected. She worked through it methodically, carefully, and then less carefully as the morning stretched and the gap between what she'd completed and what remained stayed stubbornly wide.

At noon Veer's typing slowed.

"We'll eat here today," he said, without looking up. "I'll order something up."

"Okay." She said it with a brightness she didn't entirely feel.

The food came. They ate at their desks, the lunch spot six floors up sitting empty and quiet without them. She thought about it briefly — the two chairs, the wide window, the city below — and turned back to her screen.

— — —

The afternoon was harder.

She got stuck properly around two o'clock — a section of the validation logic that didn't behave the way she expected no matter how she approached it. She stared at it. Tried three things. Stared at it again.

She glanced at Veer.

He was deep in something, brow slightly furrowed, the focused stillness of a person solving a problem that deserved his full attention.

She looked back at her screen.

I'll figure it out, she told herself. I said I could handle it.

She tried two more things. Neither worked.

The clock on her screen said 2:47.

She glanced at him again. He hadn't moved. The office around them had thinned — people finishing their parts and leaving, the crisis slowly resolving itself elsewhere on the floor. Their corner was quiet.

She looked at her screen.

She looked at her hands.

She looked at the section of logic that had defeated her for the better part of an hour and felt something heavy settle in her chest — the specific weight of having said I can handle it with confidence and then sitting in the evidence that maybe she couldn't.

He's going to think—

She stopped that thought. Started it again. Stopped it again.

Three forty-five.

She was still stuck.

She was still not asking.

— — —

She didn't know exactly when he noticed. She hadn't made a sound. But at some point the typing beside her stopped and she felt rather than saw his chair move, and then he was there — rolled casually alongside her, eyes on her screen, as if he'd simply decided to look at something in that direction.

"That one's tricky," he said. Conversationally. Like he'd walked past and noticed an interesting problem on the wall.

He leaned forward slightly. "Hmm." A pause. "If you go in from here — " he pointed, "pull the reference first, then apply the condition after rather than before — that should resolve it."

She followed what he was saying. Tried it.

It worked.

She stared at the screen.

He glanced at her — just briefly, a sideways look. "You're doing well. This isn't fresher level work."

And then he rolled his chair back and returned to his screen like nothing had happened.

Sia sat very still for a moment.

She turned back to her work.

And this time, when she got stuck — twenty minutes later, a different section, a smaller problem — she turned to him and asked. Quietly, without preamble.

He answered without looking up.

She fixed it and moved on.

Then again. And again. Each time a little easier than the last, the asking becoming less like a defeat and more like a tool she'd been refusing to pick up for no good reason.

The work moved faster. Considerably faster.

— — —

By seven the office was empty.

The floor had gone quiet around them — lights dimmed in the sections nobody was using, the hum of the building settling into its after-hours register. Outside the windows the city had switched from its daytime self to its evening one, all lit up and indifferent.

Veer finished his section at six forty. He looked at hers, looked at the clock, and without a word pulled her remaining work into a shared file and began moving through it alongside her. She noticed. Said nothing. Felt the relief and the shame arrive simultaneously and sit beside each other without resolving.

At seven twelve the last report was validated, formatted, and filed.

Veer leaned back in his chair. A long exhale — that same unwinding she'd seen yesterday, deeper tonight. He rolled his neck once and was still.

Sia stared at her screen.

Her posture had done something without her permission — shoulders drawn in, chin slightly down, the body's way of making itself smaller when the mind is being loud and unkind.

She heard his chair move.

She felt rather than saw him stop beside her. And then — light, brief, matter of fact — his hand on the top of her head. A single gentle pat, the kind that didn't ask anything or promise anything, just acknowledged that a person was there and had had a hard day.

"Come on," he said quietly. "Tag along for a bit."

She nodded. Just that.

She packed her things slowly, not looking up, and they walked out of the empty office together — her eyes on the floor, tracking the pattern of the tiles, one after another, down the corridor and into the elevator and out through the lobby and into the night air which was cooler than she expected.

She wasn't sure how long they'd been walking when she noticed they'd stopped.

She looked up.

A restaurant. Warm light through the glass, the kind of place that felt like it had been chosen rather than stumbled into. She looked at him with an expression she couldn't organize into anything coherent — what are we doing here and why and I don't deserve a nice dinner tonight all arriving at once.

"Veer—"

"We skipped the lunch spot today," he said simply. "Consider this compensation."

She looked at him for another moment. Then nodded, because she didn't have anything left to argue with.

— — —

They were seated near the glass — high floor, city spread below them, a thousand lit windows doing their quiet evening thing. The lights inside were low and warm. Somewhere behind them soft music was playing the kind that didn't demand attention.

It was, objectively, a beautiful view.

Sia looked at the table.

The menu came. They ordered. The food arrived and she ate without tasting much, which was a shame because it was probably good. Veer ate steadily, unhurried, occasionally looking out at the city with the expression of someone who finds it interesting without being impressed by it.

She was aware of how dim she must look in this room. How out of place her posture was against the backdrop of all that light.

She was trying to find something to say — an apology, an explanation, something that would address the shapeless guilt sitting in her chest — when he set down his fork.

He looked at her.

Not the sideways glance, not the brief professional look. Actually looked at her, with the calm directness he applied to things that deserved his full attention.

"Aren't you," he said quietly, "the girl from that alley?"

The world did something strange.

All of it — the guilt, the tiredness, the dim posture, the shapeless weight of the day — lifted. Just like that. Like a window opening in a room that hadn't had air in a long time.

She looked up.

Into his eyes. Calm, steady, giving nothing away and somehow everything at once.

Her face did something she couldn't have described if someone had asked her to. Happy and confused and the question she couldn't form into words — did you know, have you known, this whole time, every morning, every lunch, the game centre, all of it — written plainly across every feature.

Her mouth opened.

Nothing came out.

The city glowed quietly below them, indifferent and vast, completely unaware that something had just shifted at a corner table by the glass.
