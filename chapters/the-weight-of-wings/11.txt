She came back with her hair still slightly damp and the particular calm of someone who has stood under warm water long enough to reset.

She stood in front of the mirror.

She spent some extra minutes there. Not fixing anything — just looking. Taking stock. Turning slightly one way and then the other with the unhurried consideration of someone who has found something worth examining.

She was, she decided, looking quite good today.

This was not a thought she announced. It was a private one, held quietly in the part of her that existed before anyone else's opinion arrived. She was proud of her makeup — the small careful things she'd done that nobody would necessarily notice but that she noticed, that made the face in the mirror look like a version of herself she approved of.

She was wearing an oversized top in sky blue and white, tucked into her jeans — white, wide legged, the kind that made her look slightly taller than she actually was. Over everything a long pink jacket, full sleeves, falling to her knees, casual enough to move in and put together enough to mean something.

Minimalist. A simple golden necklace. A ring with a pattern she liked. Her grandmother's watch on her left wrist, as always.

Her mother would have had several things to say about this outfit.

She wore it anyway.

Before she left there was, of course, the photoshoot. Brief, necessary, conducted with the seriousness the occasion deserved — different angles, different expressions, one that was supposed to look candid and took four attempts. She reviewed the results with the critical eye of someone who knew what they were looking for.

Acceptable. More than acceptable.

She picked up her small handbag, dropped her phone and keys inside, and stepped out.

— — —

The city received her the way it always received her — without ceremony, without particular interest, going about its Saturday business with complete indifference to her existence.

She walked with her hands in the pockets of the pink jacket.

She was fairly certain people were looking at her. She couldn't confirm this because she was also looking at them — quick glances, calibrated, trying to catch the looking without being caught looking back. This created a kind of mutual surveillance that existed entirely in her head since nobody was actually looking at anyone, but it did make her very conscious of her walk.

She adjusted her walk.

This made it worse.

She adjusted it again.

Somewhere between the first adjustment and the third she found something that felt approximately natural and committed to it, hands in pockets, chin at a reasonable angle, holding eye contact with strangers a beat longer than she normally would before looking away.

It felt good. Whatever it was — the makeup, the outfit, the remnants of her fantasy still sitting somewhere in her posture — something was different today and she was walking in it and the city was doing nothing to discourage her.

She wasn't sure where she was going.

She kept walking.

— — —

The café had been there since she moved to this part of the city.

She had walked past it many times. Had looked through the glass at the warm light inside, the people at their tables, the particular atmosphere of a place that had been arranged to feel like somewhere worth being. She had thought — sometime. Not today. Maybe when there was a reason. Maybe when someone was with her. Maybe when it felt like less.

Today she stopped in front of it.

She looked at the door.

She went in.

The smell arrived first — coffee and something baked and the specific cool of air conditioning that had been running long enough to develop a personality. Then the light, warm and amber, coming from fixtures that had been chosen to make everything look slightly better than it was. Pictures on the walls. Music underneath the AC hum, calm and undemanding.

She found a table in the corner. Not too visible. Enough distance from the other tables to feel like her own territory.

She sat down.

She was aware, immediately and acutely, of herself sitting down. Of how she looked sitting down. Of whether sitting down in this particular way in this particular chair looked like something a person who came to cafes regularly would do or something a person who had never done this alone before would do.

She glanced around.

Everyone was doing completely normal things. Eating. Talking. Looking at their phones. Nobody had looked up when she came in. Nobody was tracking the specific way she sat in her chair or evaluating her right to be here.

She picked up the menu.

She ordered — a drink, something small to eat, pointed at the menu with the confidence of someone who had definitely been to cafes before — and then immediately picked up her phone. There were things to attend to. Important things. She scrolled with the focused expression of someone managing significant affairs, which was true in the sense that she was doing something and not just sitting alone at a table in a café she had never been to before looking like she wasn't sure what to do with her hands.

By the time her order arrived — the café had begun its work on her. Quietly, without announcing it. The music and the light and the smell of other people's food doing what good spaces do — making the person inside them forget to monitor themselves.

She leaned back in her chair.

She looked at what the table beside her had ordered and made a private assessment. Interesting. She would consider that next time.

The order arrived. She ate and drank in the particular way of someone who is genuinely enjoying something and has stopped thinking about how they look doing it — efficiently, with attention, finishing what was in front of her without drama.

Then she looked up and remembered she had to pay and leave.

The self consciousness returned immediately and completely, like it had simply been waiting in the corner this whole time.

She stood up. She was fairly certain everyone was watching her stand up. She walked to the counter — slightly faster than was strictly necessary — paid, said thank you, and moved toward the door with the focused energy of someone completing a mission.

Outside she stopped.

Stood beside the café door for a moment.

Took a breath.

Then she walked home normally. Not with the hands-in-pockets confidence of the outward journey. Just — normally. A girl who had gone to a café alone and come back out the other side.

— — —

She changed the moment she got home.

The pink jacket, the tucked top, the jeans — all carefully set aside, replaced immediately and without ceremony by an oversized t-shirt and a pair of shorts. The apartment got the real version. It always did.

She climbed into bed.

And began, with great seriousness, to debrief.

The cashier interaction. How it had gone. How it could have gone better. The exact energy she would carry next time she walked into a café — the entrance, unhurried, hands in pockets, eyes doing the right thing. The exit, slower this time, no rushing, paying with the calm of someone for whom this was entirely routine.

She was refining the exit strategy when her eyes got heavy.

She was mid thought — something about the angle of departure, the precise speed at which a person who definitely came to cafes all the time would walk toward the door — when the afternoon found her.

The full stomach. The warm room. The particular drowsiness of a Saturday that had asked more of her than expected and was now collecting its fee.

She didn't finish the thought.

She was already asleep.
