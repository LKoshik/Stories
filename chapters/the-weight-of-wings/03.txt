By eleven o'clock, Sia had read the onboarding document twice.

The second time she actually retained most of it, which she considered a personal victory given the circumstances. Her role was clearer now — data entry and report formatting to start, with the expectation that she'd move into analysis support within a month if she picked things up quickly. Standard junior trajectory. Nothing she couldn't handle.

She organised her desktop, created a folder structure that her mother would never know about and her father would never care about, and told herself firmly that she was here to work. To prove something. To be the person who showed up and delivered and didn't embarrass anyone.

She did not look sideways.

Mostly.

— — —

At 12:30 Veer's chair moved.

Sia had, against her will, become slightly attuned to the sounds of his desk — the specific rhythm of his typing, the particular way he set his coffee mug down, the almost imperceptible exhale he made when he finished a task and moved to the next one.

She told herself this was just heightened awareness from being in a new environment. Completely normal. Nothing specific about him.

He stood, opened his desk drawer, and retrieved a lunchbox. Then he looked at her.

"Did you bring lunch?"

"Yes." She held up her bag slightly.

He nodded once. "I know a spot. Quieter than the cafeteria. You're welcome to join if you'd like."

She said yes before he finished the sentence, then made herself slow down. "Sure, that sounds good."

He waited while she gathered her things, which she appreciated without saying so.

— — —

The spot was a small landing on the building's sixth floor — a wide windowsill alcove that someone had unofficially claimed years ago with two chairs and a low table, tucked into a corner where two glass walls met. The city spread out below them, indifferent and enormous. Nobody else was there.

"How did you find this place," Sia said, looking around.

"I explored the building on my first week." He set his lunchbox on the table and sat. "Most people don't go above the fourth floor unless they have a reason to."

She sat across from him and began opening her box, and for a moment they were just two people unwrapping lunch in comfortable — or at least functional — silence.

Then he glanced over.

"That looks good." A pause. "Did you cook that?"

"Yes." She felt unreasonably pleased that he'd noticed. "It's nothing special, just—"

"Would you mind?"

She blinked. He was gesturing at her box with a slightly raised eyebrow — a question, not an assumption.

"Oh — yes, please, go ahead."

He took a small portion with the kind of care that suggested he was genuinely trying not to take too much, ate it, and was quiet for a moment.

"That's really good," he said. "You cook well."

It was a simple sentence. Completely straightforward. Sia felt heat rise to her face anyway and looked down at her box.

"Ahh — no, it's not really — thank you." She pressed her lips together. Smooth, Sia. Very smooth.

She heard something. It wasn't quite a laugh — more like the suggestion of one, a slight shift in his breathing. She looked up and he had already turned back to his own lunch but there was something at the corner of his mouth that hadn't been there before.

He was amused. Quietly, privately, in a way he wasn't advertising. But amused.

"Would you like some of mine?" he asked.

"Oh — sure, just a little."

She tried it. And then she forgot to be flustered for a moment because it was genuinely, surprisingly good. Balanced and careful, the kind of food that tasted like someone had actually thought about it.

"This is delicious," she said, and meant it completely.

"Thank you. I'm still learning."

She looked at him. "You cooked this yourself?"

"Yes." He glanced at her. "Is that surprising?"

"No — I just—" She caught the slight expression on his face, that same interior amusement he wasn't quite showing. "I just asked."

He returned to his food and said nothing, which somehow made it worse and better at the same time.

They ate. The city moved below them. Someone far away was honking at something that would never honk back.

Sia turned the alley over in her mind. It was right there — the whole thing — and all she had to do was open her mouth and say I think you saved me once and it would be out in the world and she could stop carrying it around.

She took a breath.

A small sound came out — not quite a word, more like the beginning of an intention.

"Have you settled in alright?" he asked. "With the environment. Did the document make sense or are there parts you want to go over?"

The breath left her.

"It made sense," she said, after a moment. "Mostly. I had one question actually — the report formatting, there were two templates listed and I wasn't sure which one applies to the weekly summaries."

He set down his fork and explained it — clearly, without any of the slight impatience she'd learned to expect from people who knew things and didn't particularly want to explain them. He walked her through the logic of when each template was used, connected it to something she'd already read so it would stick, and stopped when she nodded instead of continuing past the point of usefulness.

She answered his follow-up question. He seemed satisfied.

He'd make a good teacher, she thought.

And then immediately caught herself thinking about him again and looked back at her lunch with the focused energy of someone very interested in rice.

They finished eating without much more conversation — comfortable in a way she hadn't expected, like silence between them didn't require filling. He packed his box neatly. She did the same. They rode the elevator back to the fourth floor and returned to their desks, and the afternoon began.

Sia opened her first actual task and started working.

Three feet to her left, Veer was already deep in his.

She did not look sideways.

For almost twenty whole minutes.
