His voice was the first thing she registered clearly.

"Sia. Good morning. Welcome to the team."

Not warm exactly. But not the opposite either. Just — level. The kind of tone that didn't perform anything, didn't reach for anything. He said it the way someone states a fact they consider worth acknowledging.

"Good morning," she said back, and was relieved to find her own voice came out normal. Completely normal. Like her ribcage wasn't doing something strange.

"Please, have a seat."

He gestured to the desk beside his — already set up, monitor on, chair pushed in neatly — and went back to his screen.

Sia sat.

She placed her bag down. Straightened it. Then straightened it again because her hands needed something to do.

She reached forward and pressed the power button and watched it blink to life, and in that particular quiet of a machine waking up slowly, she became very aware of her own heartbeat.

It was loud.

Unreasonably, embarrassingly, almost *comically* loud, the way it only got when her body decided to panic about something her brain hadn't fully processed yet. She pressed her fingers lightly against the desk and focused on the sensation of the surface — cool, solid, real — and told herself to breathe.

*It is just a person,* she thought. *It is just a desk. It is just a regular first day at a regular job.*

The desktop finished loading.

She reached for the mouse.

Then his chair moved.

He pulled it closer — not dramatically, just practically, the way someone does when they need to point at a screen — and suddenly he was right there, close enough that she could see the small details she hadn't been able to from a distance. The slight tiredness around his eyes. The plain watch on his left wrist, nothing expensive. The way he held himself — not stiff exactly, just still. Like someone who had made peace with taking up exactly as much space as he needed and no more.

"So this is the dashboard you'll be working from," he said, reaching past her to click something on the screen. "Your access is limited for now, which is standard for the first two weeks. I'll walk you through the workflow once you've had time to get oriented. Nothing complicated."

She nodded.

He glanced at her.

"You're not taking notes."

"I'm — " she blinked. "I'm memorizing."

He looked at her for a moment in a way that was impossible to read, then turned back to the screen. "The onboarding document is in the shared folder. Everything I just said is in there anyway."

He showed her two more things — where to log her hours, who to contact for IT issues — and then pushed his chair back to its original position with the same quiet efficiency with which he'd moved it.

"That's enough for now," he said, already turning back to his own work. "Take some time to settle in. First days are a lot. I'll be here if you need anything."

He said it without looking up.

Sia stared at her screen.

*He thinks I'm nervous,* she realized. *He looked at me fidgeting and holding my breath and he thought — first day jitters. New job. Understandable.*

He had absolutely no idea.

She almost laughed. She caught it just in time and converted it into a small exhale that she hoped sounded like relief.

---

For a few minutes she actually tried to focus — opened the onboarding document, scrolled through it, read the same sentence four times without absorbing a single word. Then, because she was only human and had approximately zero control over it, she glanced sideways.

He was working.

That was the only way to describe it — just *working*, with a completeness that she didn't often see in people. No phone face-down beside the keyboard that he'd check every few minutes. No leaning back and staring at the ceiling. No low-grade fidgeting, the kind everyone did without noticing, the body's way of saying *I'd rather be somewhere else.*

He was just — there. In it. Whatever was on that screen had his full attention and the rest of the world had apparently been filed away for later.

Sia had spent her whole life surrounded by people who were always half somewhere else. Her mother cooking while mentally calculating what the neighbours must think. Her father present at dinner but absent in every way that mattered. Friends who listened with one eye on their notifications.

She wasn't sure she had ever seen someone look so completely unbothered by the passage of time.

She didn't know what to do with that observation so she filed it away and turned back to her screen.

The onboarding document stared at her.

She read the same sentence a fifth time.

Outside the office windows the city continued its indifferent morning, and three feet to her left Veer worked in silence, and Sia's heart rate slowly, reluctantly, began to return to something resembling normal.
