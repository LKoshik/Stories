The afternoon passed the way first afternoons at new jobs tend to — in a mixture of small tasks and smaller uncertainties, punctuated by the particular self-consciousness of someone who is aware of being new and is trying very hard not to show it.

Sia checked her work twice before she showed it to him the first time.

"Is this formatted correctly?" She turned her monitor slightly toward him.

He glanced over, studied it for a moment. "The column headers need to be bold. Otherwise yes."

"Oh." She turned back and fixed it. "Thank you."

The second time she had a genuine question about which data set to pull from, and he answered it without making her feel like she should have known. The third time she almost didn't ask — told herself she was being too dependent, that she should figure it out — and then asked anyway because getting it wrong would waste more time than the question would.

He answered that one too. Same patience. Same economy of words.

By four o'clock she had completed her first actual task and sent it to the shared folder and felt the small quiet satisfaction of a thing done correctly. She opened the next item on her list and worked through it steadily, and somewhere in that second hour she stopped being aware of herself as the new person and became just — a person, at a desk, doing work.

It was a small shift. But it was real.

— — —

At 5:45 Veer leaned back in his chair.

It was the first time she had seen him do it — that particular lean, shoulders dropping, head tilting slightly back, a long exhale that seemed to come from somewhere deeper than just the end of a workday. Like something unwinding that had been wound for a while.

She found herself looking.

He straightened, closed whatever was on his screen, and began packing up with the same quiet efficiency he applied to everything. Sia did the same — shut down her desktop, gathered her things, checked twice that she hadn't left anything important.

They fell into step naturally, the way people do when they've occupied the same space all day and their rhythms have without discussion aligned. Down the corridor, into the elevator, the soft mechanical descent of the building releasing them floor by floor.

The lobby was emptying out around them — the end-of-day shuffle of badges and bags and people already mentally halfway home. They walked through the glass doors and the city received them, warm and loud and completely uninterested.

A few steps out Sia slowed slightly.

"Thank you," she said. "For today. I felt — " she searched for the right word. "Comfortable. More than I expected to."

He considered this for a moment, the way she was beginning to understand he considered most things — not slowly exactly, just without rushing. "I'm glad I could help." A beat. "You picked things up quickly. It made it easy."

She didn't know what to do with the compliment so she looked ahead and kept walking.

They moved together down the pavement, no particular destination yet, the city doing its evening thing around them. Then —

"What do you do?" he asked. "In your free time. Do you have hobbies?"

The question landed simply. She opened her mouth to answer it.

And then realized, with a faint internal alarm, that she wasn't entirely sure.

She thought about it genuinely — actually reaching for something, the way you reach for a word that should be right there. What did she do when she wasn't working or studying or fulfilling some obligation that had been handed to her by someone else's expectations?

"Watching movies," she said finally. It came out with the slight uncertainty of someone who has just discovered they've been answering a question they never fully examined.

"Oh." He nodded. "What kind?"

"Romantic ones. With — " she paused. "Happy endings."

"Cool," he said.

A few seconds of silence. The city filled it easily.

Then — "I'm going to a game centre. There's one about ten minutes from here." He glanced at her briefly. "You're welcome to come if you don't have plans."

She thought about it. Not long — just a second or two, the mental arithmetic of train timings and whether there was anything waiting for her at home that couldn't wait a little longer. Her apartment would be exactly as she left it. Nobody was expecting her at any particular time. The only person who'd want to know where she was was her mother, and her mother got a phone call, not a permission slip.

He waited.

He didn't say it's fine if you can't or no pressure or any of the things people said when they were already preparing for a no. He just waited, the way she was noticing he waited for most things — with a patience that didn't demand anything back.

"I don't have plans," she said. "I'd like to come."

He nodded once, as if this was a perfectly ordinary outcome, and adjusted his direction slightly.

She fell into step beside him.

Above them the sky was doing that particular evening thing it did in cities — not quite golden, not quite grey, something in between that lasted only a few minutes before it made up its mind. Sia watched it for a moment and thought about the question he'd asked her.

Do you have hobbies.

She was going to have to think about that one properly. Later, when she was alone. When there was space to be honest about the answer.

For now she had a game centre to get to and approximately ten minutes to figure out how to act like a normal person beside someone who made her heart do things she hadn't consented to.

She could work with ten minutes.

Probably.
