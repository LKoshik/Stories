Sia sat very still.

Her face couldn't settle on a single expression.

Veer waited. The way he always waited.

"I wasn't sure in the beginning," he said quietly. "But then I saw your watch. It's not easy to forget a watch like that."

Her hand moved before she thought about it — fingers closing around the worn metal strap on her left wrist. Her grandmother's watch. Old and slightly too big for her wrist and completely out of place in a world of sleek phone screens and smart devices.

She looked down at it.

For a moment her grandmother was very present at this table — in this restaurant, in this city, in this life she was trying so hard to build — and she felt the specific ache of someone who wanted to share good news with a person who was no longer there to hear it.

She looked up.

The words came out smaller than she intended, squeezed through something tight in her throat.

"Thank you." A breath. "If it weren't for you I don't know what would have happened to me."

She bowed her head. It was the only thing her body knew how to do with that much gratitude — this old, quiet gesture, more honest than anything words could carry.

Veer was still for a moment.

"I just did the right thing," he said. Simply. Receiving it and placing it gently back down. "You're welcome."

— — —

The quiet returned. Softer this time.

Outside, the city glowed beyond the glass, completely unaware.

Sia turned her grandmother's watch slowly on her wrist.

Then — "Can I ask you something?"

"Of course."

The words came out rushed, slightly breathless, like if she didn't say them fast she'd lose her nerve completely. "I saw you get stabbed by that knife. But you were fine. And I heard a — a metal sound. How did you do that?"

She looked at him with the face of someone standing at the edge of something they can't name — confused and astonished and waiting for the world to either make sense or stop pretending to.

Veer looked at her for a moment.

"You sure about that?" he said. "Not just imagining things?"

"No." Firm. "I saw it. I know what I saw."

He laughed — brief and light, something genuinely amused in it. "I'll tell you," he said. "When the time comes."

It wasn't a deflection. She understood that immediately. It was a promise — quiet and certain, the kind he probably didn't make often and didn't break when he did.

She nodded.

Filed it away, the way he filed things.

Held it quietly alongside her grandmother's watch.

— — —

"I wanted to tell you something," he said.

The shift in his voice was small but she caught it — something deliberate in it, the particular quality of words that have been considered before being spoken.

She looked at him.

"I'm not very good at communicating." He said it without self-pity, just as a fact about himself he had made peace with. "And I think because of that I may have hurt you today. So I want to apologize for that."

She opened her mouth. He continued, gently.

"The work you did today was senior level. Not fresher level. You were on your second day and you handled something that had no business being on your desk." A pause. "What I said earlier — about it not being on a fresher's level — I didn't frame it correctly. It came out wrong. The truth is that it was my work. And because of your help I was able to finish it faster than I would have alone."

He looked at her steadily.

"So thank you," he said. "For today."

And he gave her a soft smile — unhurried, genuine, the kind that reached his eyes without asking for anything back.

Sia sat with that.

She had spent the entire day coiled around her own guilt — the weight of having said I can handle it and then struggling, of having made him stay late, of having needed help she was too proud to ask for.

She had carried it through the empty office and down the elevator and through the cool night air and into this restaurant and all the way to this table.

And he had just — quietly, precisely, without drama — taken every piece of it apart and shown her what it actually was.

Not failure. Not burden.

Just two people doing difficult work together.

She didn't know what to do with that. She genuinely, completely did not know what to do with that much sincerity arriving all at once from someone she was already barely keeping her composure around.

Should she accept it? Should she say no, it was her fault for not asking sooner? Should she say something, anything, that made her seem like a person who could receive kindness gracefully?

Her eyes were doing something she had specifically told them not to do.

She blinked. Blinked again.

The restaurant blurred softly at the edges — the warm lights smearing slightly, the city beyond the glass becoming a gentle wash of gold — and she was aware in some distant part of her mind that she was about to cry in a restaurant on her second day of work in front of her senior and there was apparently nothing she could do about it.

She tried anyway.

She pressed her lips together. Focused on the table. Counted something. Tried to think about something practical and grounding like train timings or grocery lists.

And then it didn't matter anymore because she was crying — not delicately, not quietly, just fully and completely, the way you cry when you've been holding something for a long time and someone kind has finally made it safe to put down.

— — —

"Hey — " His voice, close and slightly alarmed. "Are you okay?"

Tissues appeared. She took them without looking up.

She heard his chair move. And then he was beside her, and a hand settled gently on her back — slow, steady circles, saying nothing, asking nothing, just present in the uncomplicated way of someone who has decided that staying is the right thing and doesn't need it to be more complicated than that.

She stole a glance at him through her blurred vision.

With what expression is he looking at me, she thought, even now, even through this. Does he think I am too dramatic. Does he think—

His face was calm. Not uncomfortable. Not waiting for it to be over. Just — there. Like there was nowhere else he needed to be and nothing about this moment that required fixing.

Something in her chest unknotted completely.

She stopped trying to stop.

It passed the way these things do — not dramatically, just quieter and then quieter and then she was sitting there with a fistful of tissues and puffy eyes and that specific lightness that comes after you've been carrying something too long and finally, accidentally, set it down.

— — —

They came out of the restaurant slowly.

The night air was cool and she breathed it in carefully, still slightly uneven, the remnants of crying making her sound faintly like someone with a cold. She was aware of her eyes — swollen, definitely red underneath — and had made a private peace with her appearance somewhere around the third tissue.

They walked to the station without words.

Not because there was nothing — there was everything, probably, somewhere underneath the quiet. But neither of them reached for it and somehow that was exactly right. The city moved around them and they moved through it and the silence between them was the comfortable kind, the kind that doesn't need filling.

She could feel him beside her. Just — there. The same steadiness she'd fixed her eyes on in that alley, a year ago, when her legs were shaking and the world had briefly stopped making sense.

Some things, she thought, don't change depending on the circumstances.

— — —

The station appeared. The familiar lights, the barrier, the sound of the city shifting into its underground register.

They stopped.

She turned to him, not entirely sure what she was going to say — thank you again seemed insufficient and everything else seemed like too much — and then she heard the train arriving, the low rumble of it building from somewhere in the dark.

He looked at her.

"Sia." His voice was warm — genuinely, quietly warm in a way she was still learning to receive without deflecting. "You were great today. Thank you for your hard work."

Her heart did something complicated and immediate — a burst of something she didn't have a name for yet, beating slightly wrong, slightly too fast, in a way that had nothing to do with the cold air or the long day or the walk from the restaurant.

She looked at him.

And she smiled.

Not the smile she wore for her mother's phone calls. Not the smile she put on for colleagues or interviews or family functions. Not any of the performed versions she had spent years perfecting and deploying at appropriate moments.

This one came from somewhere she hadn't visited in a long time. Maybe ever. Somewhere underneath all the proving and the performing and the constant quiet exhaustion of being what everyone needed her to be.

It came from there. And it was entirely, completely real.

"You're welcome," she said.

— — —

She went through the barrier. Found her platform. Got on the train as the doors opened and turned back one last time — puffy eyes, runny nose, the absolute wreckage of a person who had cried in a restaurant — and waved at him through the glass.

He raised his hand.

The train pulled away.

She watched him until the platform curved and he disappeared, and then she stood at the window for another moment anyway, her grandmother's watch warm on her wrist, the city sliding past outside.

Then she sat down.

Closed her eyes.

And held the evening carefully, the way you hold something you don't want to drop.
