She arrived with a runny nose and a voice that belonged to someone recovering from something.

"Good morning." Veer looked up from his screen. "Are you feeling better?"

She settled into her chair, sniffed, breathed in through her nose in the way of someone who has made peace with their current condition. "Good morning." A breath. "Much better actually. Thank you."

She smiled — warm, genuine, slightly congested — and turned to her screen.

He nodded once and returned to his work.

— — —

She had decided on the train that morning, somewhere between two stations and a particularly aggressive sneeze, that today was going to be a quiet day. Not cold, not distant — just quiet.

She had enough feelings sitting in her chest from the last two days to fill a moderately sized container and she needed some time to let them settle before adding anything new.

So if he asked her to go somewhere after work she was going to decline. Politely, warmly, with a genuine excuse she hadn't fully constructed yet but would think of by five o'clock.

She opened her tasks and got to work.

— — —

The morning passed steadily.

Something had shifted in her — not dramatically, not all at once, but noticeably. When she got stuck on something she turned to him and asked. Not immediately, not without a small internal negotiation, but the negotiation was shorter now. The voice that said figure it out yourself or he'll think less of you was still there but it had lost some of its authority. She could hear it and choose not to listen.

With him, anyway.

With the colleague two desks over who asked her to cross-check something — she smiled and said of course and then spent twenty minutes doing it in a way that was slightly more complicated than it needed to be because she didn't want to ask for clarification.

Some things changed faster than others.

— — —

At lunch they collected their boxes without discussion and took the elevator to the sixth floor.

The alcove was empty and waiting, the two chairs at their usual angle, the city spread below in its midday version — bright, busy, completely indifferent. They sat and opened their boxes and ate.

Nobody said anything.

Not about the food, not about work, not about restaurants or train stations or grandmothers' watches. Just the sound of the city below and two people eating in the particular silence of those who have recently said everything important and need some time to simply exist afterward.

She had expected it to feel awkward.

It didn't.

It felt like space. Like both of them had silently agreed that today the silence was the thing they needed and neither of them was going to apologize for that or fill it with noise just to seem normal.

They finished. Packed their boxes. Stood.

In the elevator going down she felt a small flutter of anxiety — am I being cold, is this strange, does he think I'm— and she glanced at him.

His face was completely calm. The particular stillness of someone who is not troubled by the present moment in any way. Like the silence had been exactly right and he had known it and there was nothing to examine about it.

She looked back at the elevator doors.

And let it go.

— — —

The afternoon settled into a rhythm.

And somewhere inside that rhythm, without her noticing it happening, she stopped performing.

Not all at once. In small increments — a stretch, arms above her head, the satisfying crack of her spine realigning. A yawn she didn't bother to hide behind her hand. At one point a process was running that would take several minutes and she folded her arms on the desk and put her head down, eyes closed, and simply rested.

She became aware, distantly, that she was doing this.

That she was in an office, at her desk, in front of her senior, behaving like a person in their own living room.

She waited for the anxiety to arrive.

It didn't.

She lay there a moment longer, listening to the sound of his keyboard — steady, unhurried — and thought that perhaps this was what comfort actually felt like. Not the performed version, not the I am fine and relaxed she put on for people. Just actually, simply, being somewhere without needing to manage how it looked.

The process finished. She sat up. Continued working.

Three feet away Veer typed steadily and said nothing, which was exactly right.

— — —

By five thirty the floor was thinning.

Sia began packing her things with the quiet prayer of someone hoping a specific thing would not happen. She was fond of him. She was grateful for him. She did not have the emotional capacity for another evening out and she was going to need a very good excuse in approximately —

"Sia."

She looked up. Prepared her face into something neutral.

I have plans, she thought. I have — my mother is calling, I have to — there's something I need to—

"I got something for you."

She blinked.

He reached into his bag and produced a small stack of books — thin, neat, with illustrated covers in the particular style she vaguely associated with a certain kind of Japanese comic. He held them out.

"It's the weekend," he said. "Thought you might want something to read. These are good. Knowing you, you probably haven't tried them."

She took them.

Horimiya, the cover said. A boy and a girl, drawn in clean simple lines, something quiet in the way they stood near each other.

She looked at the covers. Then at him. Then at the covers again.

She smiled — the warm, polite, slightly uncertain smile of someone who has just been handed something they are not entirely sure about but does not want to seem ungrateful. There is absolutely no way, she thought, that I am going to sit and read cartoons this weekend.

"Thank you," she said. "I'll definitely try them."

She put them carefully in her bag.

They parted ways at the building exit — a small wave, a see you Monday — and she walked toward the station with the manga sitting in her bag and the weekend sitting ahead of her and the quiet settled feeling of a day that had asked nothing of her and given her exactly what she needed.

— — —

Yesterday.

Veer's apartment received him the way it always did — quietly, without ceremony.

He dropped his bag at the door, loosened something in his shoulders that had been tight since the restaurant, and fell onto his bed with the particular surrender of someone who has been carrying a long day and has finally found somewhere to set it down.

The ceiling looked back at him, plain and patient.

He raised one arm. Looked at the ceiling through the gaps between his fingers — the light breaking into pieces, the white surface beyond, the particular stillness of a room that belongs only to you.

Am I living a better life now.

He asked it the way he always asked it. Not anxiously. Just — checking. The way you check something you want to make sure is still true.

He thought about the island. The waterfall. The sage's voice in the late afternoon light — unhurried, certain, asking him why he would give up something he hadn't yet tried. He thought about coming back to the city at twenty five with nothing but the clothes he'd packed and a decision he'd made at the edge of a cliff.

He thought about his first salary. The specific, quiet weight of something earned. How strange it had felt — to want something and work toward it instead of simply reaching out and making it appear. How much better it had felt when he arrived.

How far have I come.

Farther than the waterfall. Not as far as wherever this was going.

He tilted his head.

On the small table beside his bed — a stack of manga, worn at the corners from multiple reads. He'd collected them the way he collected most things he valued. Slowly. One at a time.

And then, from somewhere he hadn't reached for, her voice arrived.

I like romantic ones. With happy endings.

He looked at the stack.

Horimiya sat near the top. The boy and the girl on the cover, that particular quiet between them that the artist had somehow managed to draw without explaining.

Her face at the restaurant came back to him. She should enjoy her life a little more, he thought. Simply. Without examining it further.

He reached over and set Horimiya aside from the rest.

He put his arm back up. Looked at the ceiling through his fingers again.

The light came through in pieces.

He stayed like that for a while — not thinking about anything in particular, which was its own kind of good.
