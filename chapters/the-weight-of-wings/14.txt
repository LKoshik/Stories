She didn't move immediately.

The platform was emptying around her — people with places to be, auto drivers calling out destinations, a vendor setting up something at the far end — and she sat on one of the station benches with both hands flat on the seat beside her and looked at the ground.

Not thinking. Not processing. Just — still.

Her body and her mind had gotten separated somewhere between the camera click and the aunty's shoulder and the train pulling away, and they needed a moment to find each other again. She gave them that. Sat with the noise of the station moving around her and the ground staying where it was and her breath doing the thing it did when she reminded it to be deliberate.

In. Out. In. Out.

She didn't think about anything in particular.

She just sat until she felt like herself again. Approximately. Enough.

Then she stood, went to the bathroom at the edge of the platform, and washed her face. Cold water, twice. She looked at herself in the mirror above the tap — at her eyes, checking for evidence. She dried her face carefully with the edge of her dupatta.

She walked toward the exit.

— — —

Her phone rang as her foot crossed the threshold.

Her father's name on the screen.

She picked up and her voice came out exactly as she needed it to — normal, warm, slightly tired in the way that train journeys made people tired and nothing else.

"Naana, just got down. On my way."

"Ok naana come quickly." His voice, familiar and unhurried.

The call ended.

She thought about telling them.

She knew how it would go. The concern that would become questions. The questions that would become blame. Why did you sleep on a train. Was there no other place to sit. Why didn't you call someone immediately. And underneath all of it, unspoken but present — somehow, in some way, it would find its way back to her.

She put her phone in her bag.

Completely fine. Nothing much happened. Completely normal.

She practiced her voice on the way to the auto stand — a small sound in her throat, clearing it, checking the register. Checked her face in her phone screen once more. Wiped something at the corner of her eye that didn't need wiping.

The auto driver named a price.

She named a lower one.

He tried again.

She waited.

He agreed.

She got in.

— — —

The road out of the station became the road she knew.

It happened gradually — the town thinning at its edges the way her city had thinned that morning, but into something different. Not fields becoming city. City becoming village. The roads narrowing and losing their smoothness, the auto finding the rhythm of uneven mud that her body remembered without being told to. The smell arriving — that specific country smell, warm and green and faintly animal, the smell of a place that hadn't decided to be modern yet and wasn't particularly bothered about it.

Farms on both sides. A cow walking in the road with the complete confidence of something that had right of way and knew it. The auto driver honked once, patient, and went around. Dogs announcing their passage from gates and doorways, fulfilling their professional obligations.

She watched it all through the open side of the auto.

It was intimate in the way that only familiar things were intimate — not beautiful exactly, not the golden-dew-on-grass spectacular of the early morning fields. Just known. Just hers. The particular comfort of a place that had existed before you and would go on existing in exactly this way regardless of anything that happened to you on a train.

She felt something in her chest loosen slightly.

She was getting closer.

— — —

She saw the house before the auto fully stopped.

Single floor, white walls, a terrace above where someone had left a plastic chair that had been there for years. Built in the way of Indian homes from that era — not for aesthetics, not for any particular vision, just for people. Enough rooms, enough walls, enough roof. It had held her family for decades without complaint.

The sound of the auto brought them out.

Her mother first, then her father appearing in the doorway behind her — and then both of them moving toward her at once with the particular energy of people who have been waiting and are now done waiting.

She got down. Paid the driver. The auto's engine faded down the road and then the road was quiet and her parents were there.

Her mother reached her first.

The hug was immediate and uncomplicated — her mother's arms around her, the smell of her mother's hair, the specific pressure of being held by someone who had held you since before you could remember. She hugged back. Her face, over her mother's shoulder, was smiling.

Her father's hand on her head when her mother released her — a pat, brief, his version of the same thing.

"How was the journey?" her mother asked, already turning to guide her inside.

"Long," Sia said. "Slept most of the way."

Her father had picked up her bag. She looked at him — at the slight roundness that had arrived in his midsection sometime in the last few years and showed no signs of leaving.

"Naana," she said, "your stomach is getting a promotion."

Her father laughed. Her mother made a sound that was pretending not to be a laugh. Sia grinned.

Her mother took her hand as they walked inside — fingers around hers, warm and certain, the grip of someone who didn't need to say anything about having missed her.

The door closed behind them.

The house received her the way it always had.

Nobody knew what had happened to her.

Everything went as usual.
