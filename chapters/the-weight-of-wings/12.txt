She came back to consciousness slowly.

Not the alarm this time — just the natural surfacing of someone whose afternoon sleep had done its job perhaps too well. The room was darker than when she'd gone under, the light outside the window having made decisions without consulting her. She lay there for a moment taking stock of herself.

Alive. Slightly disoriented. Hungry.

She got up slowly, went to the bathroom, and somewhere between washing her face and looking at herself in the mirror she decided — egg fried rice.

The decision arrived with the particular certainty of someone whose body knows what it wants before the brain has fully booted. She nodded at her reflection once and went to the kitchen.

— — —

The chopping had its own rhythm.

Cabbage first, then carrots, the potatoes, beans, the frozen peas she kept in a small container at the back of the freezer for exactly this kind of evening. She worked with the efficiency of someone who had made this enough times to stop thinking about it, knife moving steadily, the vegetables accumulating in a neat pile beside the board.

The rice went into the electric cooker. She pressed the switch and listened to it click.

She checked her seasonings. Everything where it should be.

The cooker began its work — that particular sound of the plate rattling faintly underneath, a small domestic percussion that meant something was happening and she could attend to other things.

She went to the leftover dishes in the sink.

— — —

She was done with the dishes before the rice was done with itself.

She stood at the kitchen counter and looked out the window.

It was that kind of evening — the cloudy kind, the cool kind, the kind where the sky couldn't decide between afternoon and night and had settled into a grey in-between that let the temperature drop without announcing it. A breeze came through the window, not cold exactly but cool enough to notice, carrying the smell of the city doing its evening things.

Inside, her warm lights held their ground.

She watched the darkness creep in gradually — not arriving all at once but accumulating at the edges, filling the corners, the warm light from her lamps pushing back in a way that felt almost personal. The room became its own small universe, lit and warm, the world outside going grey and cool beyond the glass.

It was, objectively, a perfect atmosphere for a horror movie.

She considered this.

Then she considered the specific feeling of watching a horror movie alone in a small apartment on a cloudy Saturday evening with no one to grab and the darkness already doing things at the edges of the room.

She passed on that. Wisely, she told herself. Not because she was scared.

She opened her laptop and searched for something to watch. Nothing in particular in mind. She scrolled for a while with the focused expression of someone who would definitely find something good in a moment, kept scrolling, found nothing that felt right, scrolled some more, and eventually arrived at YouTube with the quiet acceptance of someone who had lost a negotiation with themselves.

She put on something about interesting facts.

— — —

The rice cooker made a sound.

She got up, opened it, transferred the rice to a wide bowl and set it under the fan to dry — spreading it with a spoon, turning it once, the steam rising and dissipating in the cool air from the window. She watched it for a moment.

Then she wandered.

Not with purpose — just the particular aimless circuit of someone waiting for something they can't fast forward. Kitchen to window to bed to kitchen again. She picked up her phone and put it down. Straightened something on the table that didn't need straightening.

The rice dried.

She was standing at the window looking at the street below when her phone rang.

Her mother's name.

She picked up.

They exchanged pleasantries in the practiced way of people who speak regularly and have developed a comfortable shorthand for it. Yes she was fine. Yes work was good. She mentioned the fried rice — told her mother she was making egg fried rice for dinner.

"Don't eat such rice too frequently," her mother said. "Not good for health. You'll become fat."

"I know," Sia said.

"Actually—" her mother's voice shifted slightly, a particular tone that preceded information she had been meaning to convey. "I kept forgetting to tell you. Tomorrow there's a family function. Everyone is coming. You should come."

Sia opened her mouth.

The old version of herself — the one from a week ago, maybe two — had an excuse ready. It lived somewhere near the front of her mind, always prepared, honed by years of practice. Something about work, something about prior plans, something that was both believable and impossible to argue with.

She didn't reach for it.

Instead she thought about it. Actually thought — the function, the travel, the people, chittappa somewhere in the mix with his opinions about her life choices. She thought about all of it and then she thought about a café she'd walked into today for no reason, and a photoshoot she'd conducted for nobody, and a fantasy she'd enacted alone in her apartment until her toe had introduced itself to the table.

"Okay," she said. "I'll come."

A beat of silence.

Her mother's voice when it returned was slightly warmer than usual. The particular warmth of someone who had expected to argue and found they didn't have to.

They worked out the details — timing, which function, what to wear, what to bring. To be there by nine she'd need to leave the apartment by six, which meant being up by five, which meant the afternoon nap was going to have consequences she would deal with later.

She hung up.

Stood there for a moment.

Then she thought about chittappa and everyone who would be there and all the things that got said at family functions and the particular specific exhaustion of performing for an entire room of people who had known her since birth and had fully formed opinions about what her life should look like.

She had agreed very easily for someone who found these things very difficult.

I should have said no, she thought.

Then she shook her head. Physically, once, the way you dislodge something that's taken up space without permission.

What will happen will happen.

She went back to the kitchen.

— — —

The egg fried rice came together the way it always did when she wasn't overthinking it — the oil, the eggs scrambled first and set aside, the vegetables going in by order of how long they needed, the rice last, everything moving in the wok with the particular rhythm of someone who has done this enough times to trust their hands.

She tasted it from a spoon.

Then she took a small amount directly into her palm, blew on it carefully until the heat became manageable, and put it in her mouth.

Before the full taste even arrived her face had already decided.

It's good, she said, to no one.

She was satisfied. She transferred it to a bowl, took it to the laptop, and sat down to eat.

The interesting facts video continued — someone was explaining something about deep sea creatures that she would have no use for and found completely absorbing. She ate steadily, one bite at a time, and the facts kept coming, and somewhere between the second and third interesting fact her mind drifted back to the function.

Chittappa. The aunties. The cousins with their careers and their timelines.

She looked back at the screen. Deep sea creatures. Very interesting.

The questions that were really assessments dressed in the grammar of concern. The specific performance of being Sia at a family function — capable, composed, not embarrassing anyone.

She took another bite. Focused on the video. Very, very interesting.

She finished her dinner. Washed the bowl and the wok and the chopping board, dried her hands, and looked around the apartment at the quiet of a Sunday evening that had been used well.

She picked up volume two of Horimiya.

— — —

The function anxiety didn't stand a chance.

She was on the sofa within minutes, legs over the armrest, back against the other side, book in the air — the same configuration as this morning, the body returning to what it already knew. The story picked up exactly where she'd left it and pulled her in without preamble.

Chittappa faded. The aunties faded. The function and its assessments and its timeline questions faded into background static and then into nothing at all.

She read.

She finished it sometime later — she wasn't sure exactly when, time having become irrelevant in the way it did when a story had her fully — and lay there afterward in the now familiar position, book held to her chest, ceiling above, the particular bittersweet fullness of a story that had ended well and left her slightly wistful about returning to a world where things were more complicated.

She set the book carefully on the table.

She turned off the light.

She was supposed to sleep easily — she'd decided this, it was logical, she had an early morning tomorrow and her body should cooperate on this one point at least.

Her body had other information.

Specifically — the nap. The long, luxurious, deeply satisfying afternoon nap that had seemed like such a good idea at the time and was now presenting its invoice. She lay in the dark wide awake, her sleep quota apparently already met, the ceiling offering nothing useful.

She shifted. Counted something. Tried the thing where you relax each part of your body separately which she had never successfully completed because she always fell asleep before she got to her feet, or didn't fall asleep at all.

Tonight she did not fall asleep before she got to her feet.

She lay there and negotiated with the darkness.

The darkness was not immediately receptive.

Some time passed — she wasn't sure how much — and then at some point, gradually and without announcement, sleep showed mercy.

It found her somewhere in the middle of a thought about nothing in particular, and she went under quietly, and a little while after that, by all available evidence, she was snoring.
