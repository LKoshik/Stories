The car rolled forward, carrying them back through the village roads.

Sia sat by the window.

The conversations around her dissolved into background noise. Fields stretched past in shades of green and gold. A few cows grazed lazily near the roadside. Somewhere ahead, a herd of goats had apparently decided that the middle of the road belonged to them entirely and were not open to discussion about it.

The car bounced gently over the uneven mud and after a while Sia's body simply settled into the rhythm of it.

For the first time that day, she wasn't expected to do anything.

No conversations.

No introductions.

No smiling.

No thinking.

Just sitting.

Every now and then a quiet sigh left her as she stared out the window.

By the time their house came into view an unexpected disappointment settled over her.

The ride was ending.

Which was unfortunate.

Because she had only just gotten comfortable.

It reminded her of Newton's First Law of Motion. A body in motion tends to remain in motion. She had learned that in eleventh grade and had not expected it to become personally relevant at a family function.

The car stopped. Everyone climbed out, thanked the driver, and headed inside.

— — —

She collapsed onto her bed the moment she entered her room.

Face up. Arms out. Completely, thoroughly done.

For a while she simply stared at the ceiling fan.

Then an important memory surfaced.

She raised one hand slowly toward it. Narrowed her eyes. Found one of the blades and focused on it with her complete and serious attention.

She began moving her hand in small careful circles.

Nothing happened.

Perhaps concentration was the issue.

She tried harder.

The fan continued to display a complete lack of respect for her efforts.

Apparently a few days ago she had watched a video claiming that certain people possessed telekinetic abilities. The video had been very convincing. She had found it credible at the time.

Meanwhile, somewhere outside her room, her parents were apparently discussing a particular well-mannered boy who had managed to earn their approval.

Sia paid no attention.

She was currently busy discovering that she had, in fact, been thoroughly gaslighted by the internet.

"Haaa."

She dropped her hand.

She got up. Changed into her comfortable clothes. Knocked on the door frame.

"Maa — I'm going to rest for a bit. Wake me up by three. I have to catch the four o'clock train."

"Ok Sia."

Sleep arrived quickly, which was considerate given the circumstances.

— — —

At three her mother woke her.

She got up, freshened herself, and changed back into the blue dress she had arrived in. Her father had already arranged an auto.

She hugged them both.

Her father walked her to the station. Found her a seat. Lifted her bag into the overhead space and adjusted it twice to make sure it wasn't going anywhere. Then he stood on the platform as the train doors closed and waved from outside the window.

She waved back.

Her father had, in the time between finding her a seat and the train departing, also befriended two families in the same compartment — a newly married couple and a young couple with a baby of about two or three years — and informed them, with complete seriousness, that they were to take care of his daughter.

His twenty two year old daughter.

Sia had studied the window with great focus during this exchange.

The families were kind. They made small conversation — where she worked, how long the journey was, the usual things. She answered warmly.

Then the baby caught her attention.

He was sitting in his mother's lap looking at everything making adorable noises. She watched him for a while.

"Can I hold him for a moment?"

His parents smiled and handed him over.

He lasted approximately four seconds before deciding against it. The crying was immediate and decisive.

She handed him back with quiet efficiency.

The parents were apologetic. She smiled and said it was completely fine, which was true in the sense that she understood it wasn't personal and not entirely true in the sense that she was thoroughly disappointed of not even being approved by a small creature who didn't know anything about expectations or timelines or IBM layoffs.

She looked out the window.

— — —

At seven the station arrived.

They got down together. The families were going the same direction for a little while and they walked together before splitting off. Before they did the couple with the baby stopped her.

"Take care on the way. If anything comes up call us immediately."

They handed her their number. She thanked them genuinely and watched them go.

Then she walked.

It was quieter here than the city — fewer people, wider gaps between the sounds. Her own footsteps. Her own breathing. She gripped her phone a little more firmly than usual and moved at a pace that was slightly faster than comfortable until her building appeared and the door closed behind her and the city was on the other side of it.

Her apartment was exactly as she had left it.

She stood in the middle of it for a moment. Took a breath. Set her bag down.

She didn't have the energy to cook and her mother had known this — there was a box waiting in her bag, rice and curry, packed with the quiet foresight of someone who had been feeding this particular person for twenty two years.

She washed herself, changed, and sat down to eat.

— — —

Her phone rang.

Her father.

She put her hand to her forehead. She had forgotten to call them.

"Aa naana — I just got in."

"Oh ok naana. Everything went smooth?"

"Ha."

"Ok. Take care then."

"Ha naana, bye."

"Wait — your mother wants to talk."

A brief pause.

"Sia, I kept the curry and rice — have it for tonight."

"Ya maa."

"After eating do some walking. Don't go straight to bed."

"Ya maa, I know."

She kept the phone on charge.

She ate the curry and rice while watching the telekinesis video again — studying it carefully this time, trying to identify where exactly her technique had gone wrong. The video remained convincing. The results remained unchanged.

She washed the box.

Her eyes went to the bed.

She thought about walking.

She remembered the walk from the station to her apartment and decided that counted.

She got into bed without guilt.

Stared at the fan.

Raised her hand one more time.

The same slow circles. The same focused attention.

Nothing.

She let her hand fall.

Maybe in the future.

Tomorrow she had office. Today had been long — longer than it looked from the outside, longer in ways she hadn't told anyone about and probably wouldn't. Her body seemed to understand this. Sleep came quickly, without the fight of the previous night.

She had a manga to return.

She had stories she wanted to share.

She was, she realized as her eyes closed, slightly looking forward to tomorrow.

Slightly.
