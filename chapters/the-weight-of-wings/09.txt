She walked faster than usual.

Not running — just that particular pace that happens when your body is slightly ahead of your brain, moving with an energy you haven't fully accounted for yet. The station wasn't far. She had taken this route enough times now to do it without thinking, which was convenient because she wasn't entirely thinking.

She was not thinking about the bag on her shoulder.

She was absolutely not thinking about what was inside it.

The train arrived. She got on, found a seat, and settled in the way she always did — bag on her lap, one hand resting on top of it. The city started moving past the window, blurring into streaks of light and colour, and she leaned her cheek against her hand and watched it go.

And then without her permission her mind went somewhere else entirely.

The lunch spot. The city below them, indifferent and vast. His voice explaining something with that particular patience that never made her feel small. The restaurant, the city glowing through the glass. The station, the train pulling away, his hand raised.

You were great today. Thank you for your hard work.

She smiled.

It arrived on her face quietly, slightly sideways, the kind of smile that belongs to a private thought and knows it. She caught it happening and didn't stop it.

Then she glanced down at her bag.

Just to check.

She unzipped it slightly, enough to see the corner of the cover — the boy and the girl, clean simple lines — and zipped it back up.

Still there.

She looked back out the window.

— — —

The apartment building was a ten minute walk from the station, up a street that the city hadn't gotten around to making interesting yet — functional, a little tired, the kind of block that housed people who were between one life and the next. Her building was on the left, four floors, an elevator that worked most of the time.

She took the stairs.

Third floor, second door on the right. She found her key, unlocked it, and pushed it open.

The room greeted her the way it always did — with complete honesty.

It was small. One room that held everything — a bed pushed against the left wall, a kitchen along the right that was more of a counter with ambitions, a door in the back that led to the bathroom. A window above the kitchen that let in the afternoon light when it felt like it.

And everywhere, in the comfortable way of a space that belonged only to its owner — things. A jacket draped over the back of the chair that had been there since Tuesday. Two pairs of shoes near the door, not quite put away. The study table along the wall buried under a particular geology of papers, chargers, a mug she kept meaning to wash, notes she kept meaning to file.

The bed had clothes on it. Not dirty — just present. The particular accumulation of someone who got dressed in a hurry most mornings and dealt with the consequences later.

She navigated all of it without looking, the way you move through a space you know in your bones. Dropped her bag by the door. Toed off her shoes and nudged them approximately toward the other pair.

Then she picked up the bag again, took out the manga, and looked around for somewhere to put it.

The table was its usual self.

She pushed things aside with her forearm — not tidying, just relocating — and cleared a small deliberate space. Set the volumes down carefully, covers up, in a neat stack that was slightly incongruous with everything around it.

She looked at them for a moment.

Then she turned around and went to start on the chores.

She was halfway through the dishes when her phone rang.

Her mother's name on the screen. She dried her hands and picked up.

"Sia."

"Hi Amma."

"How was work this week? Are they treating you well? Is the pay on time? Have you eaten properly? You sound tired. Are you sleeping enough?"

She answered in order, efficiently, with the particular tone she used for these calls — warm, reassuring, slightly edited. Work was good. Yes they were treating her well. Pay came on the first. She was eating fine. She wasn't tired. She was sleeping enough.

All of these were approximately true.

Her mother talked for a while about various things — a cousin's engagement, something a neighbour had said, a reminder about a family function next month that Sia had already noted and was quietly dreading. She listened and responded at the right moments, stirring the conversation along, performing the role of the daughter who was doing fine and not worrying anyone.

It was almost comfortable. She had been doing it long enough.

And then her mother said — "Hold on, Chittappa wants to speak to you."

The specific weight of those words.

She had heard this exact handoff at every family gathering since she was sixteen — Chittappa wants to speak to you — followed by the same lecture, the same comparisons, the same disappointment wrapped in concern.

Her uncle's voice came on — her mother's older brother, settled and certain in the way of someone who had decided long ago that his life was the correct template and everyone else was a variation. He asked about the job. She told him. He made a sound that wasn't quite approval.

"Data entry," he said. "That's what you're doing after all that studying."

"It's an entry level position. It's standard to—"

"Your cousin Priya started as an analyst. From day one." A pause. "Of course she went to a better college. These things matter, Sia. Your mother doesn't say it but I will — you need to be thinking about where this is going. A girl your age, living alone in the city, doing data entry. What is the plan exactly?"

She kept her voice level. "I have a plan."

"I hope so. Because nobody is going to wait forever. Career, life, everything — there's a timeline. I'm saying this for your own good. Your parents worry. We all worry."

She said the right things. She always said the right things.

When the call ended she put the phone down on the counter and stood there for a moment, hands flat on the surface, looking at nothing in particular.

Your cousin Priya.

A better college.

Nobody is going to wait forever.

She had heard versions of this her whole life. She knew the shape of it by heart, knew exactly where each word was going to land before it arrived. And still, every single time, it found the same places. The same soft spots underneath all the composure.

She wasn't hungry anymore.

She left the dishes half done. Left the rest of the chores. Walked to the bed, moved the clothes to one side without folding them, and lay down.

The ceiling looked back at her.

She lay there with the weight of the call sitting in her chest — not sharp, not dramatic, just the dull familiar heaviness of being measured against something she hadn't chosen and found slightly wanting.

Of being worried about in a way that felt nothing like care.

After a while she reached for her phone.

She opened the first video that appeared — something silly, a cat doing something cats shouldn't be able to do — and watched it without really watching it. Then another. Then another. The algorithm understood what she needed and delivered accordingly, an endless scroll of things that asked nothing of her and required no feeling in return.

Gradually, in the way that only distraction can manage, the heaviness in her chest loosened slightly. Not gone. Just quieter.

She lowered the phone.

Her eyes drifted to the table.

The manga sat in its careful stack, slightly incongruous among everything else, the boy and the girl on the cover looking the way they always looked — like two people standing near each other with something unspoken between them.

She glanced at it again, longer this time. The way Veer had held it out to her, like it was nothing and everything at once.

She reached over without thinking too much about it.

She started sitting upright, back straight, legs out in front of her. Responsible. Measured. Giving this a fair and considered try.

By page twenty she was on her stomach, legs bent up behind her, chin propped on one hand.

By page forty the pillow had migrated under her chest and she was holding it there without knowing when that had happened.

She was already rooting for them. She hadn't planned to root for them — she'd planned to read this politely and report back to Veer that yes she'd tried it, very nice, thank you — but somewhere in the first thirty pages they had gotten their hands into her without announcing it and now she was completely, helplessly invested.

There was a moment — she had to put the book down.

Just for a second. Just to breathe.

Because Hori had just shown Miyamura something real about herself. Not the version she showed everyone else. The actual version. And he had looked at it without flinching and just — stayed. Like it was the most natural thing. Like seeing someone clearly was not a complicated act.

Sia picked the book back up.

She kept reading.

At the parts where something was about to happen — where the tension pulled tight and the page turn felt like a held breath — her legs started moving on their own. A kind of anticipation her body couldn't contain, feet in the air, the pillow pressed harder to her chest.

She flipped the page.

Then she rolled onto her back, book held up above her face, ceiling and story competing for her attention. The ceiling lost. She kept reading in this position for a while, arm occasionally getting tired, switching hands, the story moving through her in that specific way that good stories do — not loudly, not dramatically, just steadily, rearranging things slightly as it went.

She migrated back. Forward. Sideways. The bed became a reading landscape, blanket pushed to one corner, pillow relocated twice, hair coming loose from whatever it had been in without her noticing.

And then she turned the last page of the first volume.

She lowered the book slowly.

Lay there. Back on the bed. Hair spread out in a halo of comfortable chaos around her head. The book held against her chest with both arms, pulled close, like keeping it there meant keeping the warmth of it inside her a little longer.

She stared at the ceiling.

It was late. The room was quiet in the particular way of late nights in small apartments — the city outside reduced to a distant murmur, the building settling into itself.

The ceiling looked back at her, plain and patient.

And she lay there with something that wasn't quite sadness and wasn't quite joy — something in between, bittersweet and soft, the feeling of having been shown a window into a life and realizing you'd been standing with your back to it for a very long time.

I missed so much.

Not dramatically. Not as a cry. Just — quietly, honestly, the way you acknowledge something true when you're alone and there's nobody to perform for.

She held the book a little tighter.

After a while she reached over and set it carefully on the table — not far, just within reach — and turned off the light.

Volume two could wait until tomorrow.

She closed her eyes.

Her last thought, half formed and already dissolving into sleep, was of a table beside a bed somewhere across the city, and a stack of books worn at the corners, and someone who had looked at her and known — quietly, without making anything of it — exactly what she needed.

She was asleep before the thought finished.
