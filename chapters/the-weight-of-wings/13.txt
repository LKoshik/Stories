She woke up before the alarm.

Not dramatically — no sitting bolt upright, no sudden clarity. Just eyes opening in the dark of the early morning, the ceiling familiar and the room quiet, and the knowledge somewhere underneath everything that today required her to get up and so she was getting up.

She went to the bathroom. Showered. Got ready with the focused efficiency of someone who has a train to catch and has already decided not to waste anything on hesitation.

The bag was packed the night before — a medium sized laptop bag, cute enough to not look like an emergency kit, practical enough to hold everything she'd need. Laptop. Charger. Makeup set for when she arrived. A water bottle. Two packets of chips because she knew herself and her stomach and the particular demands of a three hour journey on an early morning.

She was wearing a simple blue dress — nothing that drew attention, nothing that invited a second look. Her mother would put her in a saree the moment she arrived. That battle was already decided and not worth fighting before it started. For now — comfortable, simple, practical.

The grandmother's watch on her wrist. No other jewelry. She'd learned early that certain things you didn't wear on trains, especially alone, especially in the early morning when the compartments were empty and the city hadn't fully woken up yet.

She locked the door. Checked the handle.

Once.

She made the train with time to spare.

— — —

The general compartment was mostly empty at that hour.

She stood at the door for a moment and assessed — the main section with its rows of three seats facing each other, already occupied by a few sleeping bodies and one man eating something from a tiffin. The smaller section on the other side, two seats facing each other, one beside the window.

She went to the window seat without deliberating.

She settled in, opened the window, and placed her bag on her lap with both hands resting on top of it. The train began to move — that familiar lurch and then the gradual building of rhythm, the platform sliding away, the city beginning to thin at its edges.

The breeze came through the open window immediately. Cool and slightly damp, the early morning air that existed before the heat had gotten around to it. She rested her head against the window frame and watched the city become its outskirts and then become something else entirely — fields, farms, the particular green of rural morning that the city never managed to replicate. Cows grazing in the low light. The sun just clearing the horizon, that specific gold that landed on the dew on every blade of grass and plant and made the whole landscape briefly, quietly spectacular.

It was the kind of thing you forgot existed until you were looking at it.

The train swayed. The breeze moved through her hair. The fields kept coming, unhurried and vast.

Sleep arrived without asking — barged in, as it tended to do, to collect the quota it hadn't gotten the night before. She didn't fight it. Her eyes closed somewhere between one field and the next and the rhythm of the train carried her under.

— — —

Two hours passed.

She surfaced slowly — the particular return to consciousness of someone who has slept well and unexpectedly, the mind reassembling itself piece by piece. Sound first. Then light through her eyelids. Then the awareness of where she was.

There were sounds around her that hadn't been there before.

Laughter. Low and pointed, the kind that was performing itself for an audience.

And a click.

The specific, unmistakable click of a phone camera.

Her eyes opened.

The world snapped into focus with a jolt that went from her head to her feet in a single instant — a current of adrenaline that her body had already been running before her brain caught up.

There was a guy sitting directly across from her. Young. Wearing an expression that she understood and wished she didn't and two in the side seats, one holding a phone. All looking at her. Laughing.

She understood immediately.

Her mind went blank.

Not thought — the opposite of thought. The alarm firing so completely that everything else evacuated. Her heart was wrong. Too fast, too high, somewhere in her throat. Her legs had lost their substance — not shaking exactly, just absent, like the connection between her intention and her body had been briefly severed.

She put on a face. Anger. Disgust. She assembled it because it was the only thing she had.

Window. Phone. Window. Phone.

Don't look at them.

Your stop is soon. You'll be there in no time. Just bear with it, it happens, don't worry, you are here, you are fine, don't worry.

Then words started. Comments. The kind that landed like objects thrown without needing to be clever. She pressed closer to the window. Her grip on the bag tightened until she could feel the seams against her palms.

She thought about getting up.

She thought about what might happen when she stood.

She stayed.

And then the one across from her stood up.

Everything stopped.

Her heart didn't accelerate. It did something worse. A single enormous missed beat. Sweat at her forehead. Her bag gripped so hard her knuckles had gone beyond feeling.

She was still, completely, helplessly still — paralyzed in the specific way that fear paralyzes, not from the outside but from the inside, her own nervous system holding her in place.

She wanted to shout.

Her voice wasn't there.

Her body wasn't there.

He took a step.

She heard a sound — sharp, immediate, the flat crack of something fast and certain.

The bump didn't come.

She looked up.

— — —

There was a woman standing in the aisle.

She was not particularly large. She was not young. She was wearing a cotton saree and had the kind of face that had spent decades forming opinions about things and wasn't shy about it. Her hand was still in the position it had been in a half second ago — extended, follow through complete.

The man was holding his cheek.

The woman looked at the three of them with eyes that contained something Sia had never quite seen before — fury that had no fear in it whatsoever. Not performing bravery. Just genuinely, completely unafraid. Like the concept of being intimidated by these three had not and would not occur to her.

She held that look for a moment that lasted longer than it should have.

Then she reached down, took Sia's arm, and pulled.

Sia followed. She didn't decide to follow — her body just went, the way it goes toward safety when safety presents itself and the reasoning brain is still offline. Through the section, past the man still holding his cheek, into the next compartment.

— — —

The woman's daughter was there — a girl around Sia's age who looked up when they entered with the expression of someone who didn't know exactly what had happened but could read the information clearly enough from the two faces in front of her. She moved without being asked, creating space on the seat.

The woman sat beside Sia.

She took Sia's hand — not gently, not tentatively. Fingers interlocked, a grip that was firm and certain and said something that wasn't in words. Something that didn't need to be.

I am here. You are safe. Be strong.

She didn't speak. She was still furious — Sia could feel it in the set of her jaw, the quality of her silence. The fury wasn't at Sia. It was just present, held alongside the hand-holding, the two things existing together without contradiction.

Sia stared at their interlocked fingers.

And something that had been held very tightly inside her chest since the moment the camera clicked began, without her permission, to come apart.

The tears arrived quietly at first. Then less quietly. She was crying in a train compartment beside a stranger who was holding her hand and she couldn't stop and she was aware of ruining the woman's saree and couldn't stop that either.

The woman's free hand came up — slowly, without startling her — and touched her cheek. Gentle. Then she pressed Sia's head, carefully, onto her shoulder.

Her hand moved to Sia's hair.

It's okay beta. It's okay. You're fine now. They are shameless creatures. I am here. You're fine.

The daughter held out a handkerchief without a word.

Sia took it. Pressed it to her face. The fabric smelled like something clean and familiar — the specific smell of someone's home carried on their things — and for some reason that made the crying worse before it made it better.

She cried until she stopped. It happened gradually — quieter and then quieter and then just the uneven breathing of afterward, her head still on the woman's shoulder, the woman's hand still moving slowly through her hair.

She sat up.

She looked at the woman.

There was no adequate expression for what she felt — gratitude was too small a word and everything larger felt too large for this moment, this train, this compartment. She took the woman's hand in both of hers and pressed it, bowing her head over it.

"Thank you aunty. Thank you."

Her voice broke on the second thank you.

The woman made a sound — something between a tsk and a sigh — and pulled her into a hug. Arms around her, hand rubbing her back, the specific warmth of someone who has decided you need to be held and is doing it without making it complicated.

It's okay. It's okay. Don't fear them. They are stupid, they can't do anything to you. Stay strong beta.

They pulled apart.

The woman asked her name. Started talking — about where she was going, about her daughter, small things, ordinary things, the kind of conversation that had nothing important in it and was therefore exactly what was needed. Sia answered. Listened. Felt herself returning to herself, slowly, the way you return to a room after a power cut when the lights come back on — everything where it was, just briefly darker than you remembered.

— — —

The scenery outside became familiar.

Her station.

She stood, gathered her bag, and turned to the woman with everything she hadn't been able to say sitting somewhere in her expression.

The woman smiled — wide, warm, the smile of someone who needed nothing back and was giving anyway — and patted her back twice.

Sia got off from the rear door.

She didn't cross their compartment.

She stood on the platform as the train began to move and looked back at the door. The woman was there, one hand on the frame, watching her.

Sia raised her hand.

The woman waved back.

The train took her away — past the platform edge, past the signal post, around the gentle curve of the track — and then she was gone, and Sia was standing on the platform of her hometown station with her laptop bag and her chips and her grandmother's watch, the morning sun already warmer here than it had been in the city.

She stood there for a moment.

Then she took a breath.

And walked toward the exit.
