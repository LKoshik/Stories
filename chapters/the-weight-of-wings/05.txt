The game centre announced itself before they reached it — a warm spill of light onto the pavement, the muffled suggestion of sound from inside, the kind of glow that looked almost inviting if you were the type of person who had ever considered going into one.

Sia had never been the type of person who had ever considered going into one.

Such a waste of money, she had thought, on the rare occasions she'd passed places like this. Who are all these people and what are they doing in there.

She was about to find out.

Veer held the door.

She walked in and stopped.

It was loud but not assaulting — a layered kind of noise, machines and music and the particular energy of people genuinely enjoying themselves. Screens everywhere, bright and animated, pulling the eye in six directions at once. People in pairs and groups, laughing, groaning, competing.

A smell of something fried somewhere. The ceiling was higher than she expected and strung with lights that made the whole place feel like its own contained universe, separate from the city outside.

She had wandered past places like this her whole life and constructed an entire opinion about them without once stepping inside.

"First impression?" Veer asked.

She realized she was still standing near the entrance. "It's — bigger than I thought."

"It gets better." He moved forward and she followed, falling into step behind him as he navigated the space with the ease of someone who had been here before, scanning the machines with a particular focused interest she was beginning to recognize as his version of enthusiasm.

He stopped at a racing setup — two seats, two steering wheels, screens curved wide enough to actually feel like a windshield.

"Have you driven before?" he asked.

"I have a licence."

"That's not what I asked."

She paused. "I've driven approximately four times."

He almost smiled. "Perfect. Get in."

— — —

She lost. Comprehensively, repeatedly, without mercy.

But the thing was — and this surprised her more than anything else about the evening — she didn't care. Not even slightly. Because somewhere around the third race something had shifted and she had stopped being a person self-consciously doing a new thing and started being a person actually in it, hands tight on the wheel, leaning into corners that didn't require leaning, making sounds she would later prefer not to think about.

"NO — " she said, as his car cut inside her on a bend and pulled ahead.

"You braked too early."

"I didn't brake, I — " the car hit a barrier. "That was your fault somehow."

"Statistically unlikely."

"I don't care about statistics right now Veer—"

She heard him laugh. Actually laugh — brief and genuine, surprised out of him by something she'd said, and it was so different from the quiet interior amusement she'd catalogued so far that she almost lost track of the race entirely.

Almost.

"Oh come ON," she said, as he crossed the finish line ahead of her for the fourth time.

He leaned back in his seat. "You're getting faster."

"I'm going to beat you."

"You almost did on that last one."

She hadn't almost done anything on that last one and she suspected he knew it, but she appreciated the lie enough not to challenge it.

— — —

The zombie shooter was his second suggestion and she agreed immediately, which she slightly regretted the moment the opening sequence started and something lurched out of the darkness on screen making a sound that had no business existing.

"Oh — " she said.

"You're fine."

"I know I'm fine I just — " something else appeared. She shot it approximately seventeen times. "Is it dead?"

"It's been dead for a while."

"I'm making sure."

He was better at it than her — controlled, deliberate, covering angles she didn't think to cover. She compensated by shooting everything at maximum speed and accepting that accuracy was a secondary concern. It worked, mostly. When it didn't she made sounds that she was choosing not to be embarrassed about because there was no space for embarrassment when something was running at her face on a large screen.

At one point she grabbed his arm without thinking.

She let go immediately.

He said nothing, just redirected her attention to something on the left that needed shooting, and she shot it six times and they moved on and that was that.

— — —

They found a small table near the edge of the floor, away from the loudest machines. Sia went for the drinks — at least let me do this she had said, and he had accepted without argument — and came back with two cold bottles and a bag of something fried that she had acquired almost unconsciously because it smelled correct.

"This was a good idea," she said, sitting down.

He looked at her. "The snacks or the evening?"

"Both." She opened her drink. "I've never done this before. Any of it."

"I know." He said it without judgment, just as a fact he had observed. "You looked like someone seeing something for the first time."

"Was it obvious?"

"Only at the beginning."

She considered this. "What did I look like at the beginning?"

He thought about it for a moment. "Like someone revising an opinion they'd held for a long time."

She laughed — a real one, unguarded. "That's accurate actually."

They sat with their drinks and the noise of the place moved around them comfortably. She ate something fried and looked at the screens and felt, in a way she couldn't entirely account for, completely at ease. Not the performed ease of someone trying to seem relaxed. Actually, simply, easy.

It was an unfamiliar feeling.

She decided she liked it.

— — —

It was getting late. They gathered their things and moved toward the exit, and the city received them again — cooler now, the evening having made its decision about what kind of night it wanted to be.

"Next time," Sia said, "I'm going to beat you at the racing one."

"You're welcome to try."

"I've been thinking about it actually. You always take the inside line on the third corner — if I hold the outside and cut late I can take it from you on the straight."

He glanced at her. "You've been strategizing."

"I've been analyzing. There's a difference." She was walking slightly faster now, the evening's energy still in her. "And the zombie one — I think my technique was actually more efficient than yours. High volume, wide coverage, minimal hesitation."

"You shot a lamp post for eleven seconds."

"It was a suspicious lamp post."

He was smiling. Not the interior kind, not the suggestion of one — an actual smile, unhurried and genuine, directed somewhere slightly ahead of them as they walked. She saw it in profile and noted it the way you note something you want to remember.

They reached the station. The entrance light fell across them and the city moved around them and she turned to face him.

"Thank you," she said. "For today. Both halves of it."

"I had a good time," he said. Simply, without decoration.

She went through the barrier, found her platform, and when she looked back from the top of the stairs he raised a hand. She waved back — probably too enthusiastically, she'd think later — and went down to wait for the train.

— — —

It arrived. She got on. The doors closed.

The train pulled away from the platform and she moved to the window and could just make him out still at the top of the stairs, and she waved again through the glass like a complete person, and he raised his hand again, and then the train curved away and he was gone.

She stood at the window for another moment anyway.

Then she sat down.

Then she replayed, in full, everything she had said in the last fifteen minutes.

I've been analyzing. There's a difference.

It was a suspicious lamp post.

The killer moves speech. The strategy breakdown. The part where she had grabbed his arm during the zombie game and then let go very quickly and hoped he hadn't noticed, which he definitely had, because he noticed everything.

Her face did something warm and complicated.

With what face, she thought, am I supposed to walk into that office tomorrow.

But underneath the embarrassment — underneath all of it — something else was sitting quietly, solid and warm and entirely unfamiliar.

She had not performed anything tonight. Not once. She had just — been there. Loud and unfiltered and genuinely herself, and the world had not ended, and he had smiled, and it had been the best evening she could remember having.

Maybe ever.

Maybe ever felt dramatic. She sat with it anyway and decided it was true.

— — —

The apartment was exactly as she'd left it. She changed, cooked dinner — humming something tuneless and slightly embarrassing, stopping when she caught herself and then starting again because there was nobody to hear it — and ate, and cleaned up, and did all the small domestic things that usually felt like tasks and tonight felt like nothing at all.

She lay down.

Pulled her pillow close.

Stared at the ceiling with the particular expression of someone whose face hasn't decided what to do yet.

His smile. Walking beside her. The way he'd waited at the top of those stairs.

Tomorrow, she thought.

She was, against all reasonable expectation, excited for tomorrow.

She closed her eyes.

She was asleep before she finished the thought.
