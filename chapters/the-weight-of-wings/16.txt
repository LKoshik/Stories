She looked up.

She looked up.

Her body knew before her mind did — a jolt, head to toe, her chest tightening in the specific way it tightened when something she had been hoping to avoid was now standing directly in front of her.

Chittappa.

She stood up immediately. Forced a smile — the kind that said I am very happy to see you with such commitment that only someone paying close attention would notice the hand gripping the end of her saree.

"Eating all by yourself, Sia!" A laugh. The sarcastic kind, the kind that knew exactly what it was doing.

She gave a mild laugh back, slightly bowing, one hand holding the plate, the other finding the edge of the saree. "Not like that uncle." She gestured at the chair. "It was empty."

He looked at her properly for a moment.

"You look really pretty today. Your mother's magic?"

"Haha." She nodded. The smile held. The hand on the saree tightened slightly.

— — —

Chittappa was tall — not exactly fit but healthy, the kind of healthy that came from decades of regular meals and no particular effort. A golden white half-sleeved cotton shirt, black cotton pants, two rings on his right hand with green and red gems catching the light. A large moustache, clean shaved below it. A few remaining hairs on top of his head that appeared too attached to him to consider leaving.

He looked at his plate and began mixing his rice and curry — the gesture of someone settling in for a casual conversation, no urgency, nowhere else to be.

Sia's smile needed fixing every few seconds.

He ate a little.

"Mmmh." He looked up. "This is good, isn't it, Sia?"

"Yes uncle."

He nodded as if this had been expected and continued mixing. Chewing. Considering.

"Hmm." A pause. "How's life?"

She wasn't entirely sure what he wanted to know from that question. "It's going good uncle."

Her free hand had been assigned to the plate. The other hand was communicating its feelings directly to the saree.

Please go away.

"Is everything okay at work? Getting along well?"

"Yes uncle. I just joined so I'm slowly getting to know everyone."

Go away.

"Hm. Hm."

He finished what was in his mouth, nodding at his plate.

Then his tone shifted — something in it that found the specific frequency of Sia's nerves and settled there.

"I have already said this to you from the beginning, Sia."

"Communication is very important. More than skill, people value communication."

"You have to prove yourself to everyone. Whatever you do, it doesn't matter — you have to be the best at it."

Veer crossed her mind for a moment. The office. His non-performing nature — the complete absence of the need to announce himself to anyone. She knew there was a different world that chittappa didn't know about and couldn't even begin to comprehend.

"Yes uncle."

Really. In a function.

He showed no signs of stopping. This was routine — she knew this routine, had known it for years. He was one of those people who kept talking completely unaware of the person they were talking to, nodding to himself, agreeing with himself, thoroughly convinced he was doing something useful.

He continued mixing his rice.

She stood there — plate going cold, smile requiring maintenance, everything she was holding packed into a box while the universe sent chittappa to kick the lid repeatedly.

He stopped mixing. Looked up as if remembering something.

"Do you know Varun? He got placed at Infosys. Did you speak with him?"

Varun. Her distant cousin — they had gotten along well during school and college summer holidays, easy company, the kind of person she actually liked talking to. Then they had both moved to different cities for college and lost touch the way people lost touch. She had thought of him occasionally. She would have been happy to reconnect.

Just not like this. Not as someone who called because he got a job.

"I know him," she said. "But I haven't talked to him recently."

She gripped the saree a little harder. Her eyes made a brief sweep of the room searching for anything — a small emergency, a distraction, someone calling her name — and found nothing.

"Don't talk, how will you know anything. Building connections is very important. You don't know the value of what I'm saying now but you will understand later."

"Call and talk with him. Find out how he did it."

"Yes uncle."

The same judgements from someone who didn't know her. The frustration was leaking out in small moments — a twitch at the corner of her expression, something in her eyes — but he was focused on his food, nodding to himself. I am doing the right thing.

"You should—"

He was not stopping.

It reached the boiling point the way these things reached boiling points — not suddenly, but inevitably, the heat having been building long enough that the only surprise was that it had taken this long.

She imagined throwing her plate.

She imagined ending the conversation with a look that said exactly what she thought and walking away before he could respond.

Her mind held her. Barely. The calculation running automatically — the consequences of one moment of lost composure versus the cost of tolerating the next few minutes.

She knew the answer. She always knew the answer.

She thought about what Veer would do in this situation.

It was clear as a day. He would genuinely listen. He would accept the parts that were true without performance and ask questions about the parts he didn't understand. He wouldn't lose his composure — it wouldn't even occur to him to lose it.

She knew she wasn't Veer.

She knew Veer didn't have a chittappa.

But she gathered what she had and let everything else go to deaf ears.

"—know all these things, Sia. Now internet is there, you don't need anyone to guide you. We didn't have it in our time and we struggled a lot. But you are very fortunate."

"Yes uncle."

The heat was in her cheeks now. The makeup held it in. The same dialogues, the same conversation, the same conclusion it always reached.

She let go of the saree. Transferred the plate to her other hand and began eating — head nodding, eyes somewhere neutral, the automatic performance of a person receiving information.

Chittappa did not stop. He moved through everything — the AI crisis, the job disruptions, specific companies, specific numbers, how many people were hired and how many were let go and what it all meant for someone in her position. She nodded to all of it. Accepted that she knew nothing about any of it. Did not have the patience to do otherwise.

Somewhere in it she lost the last of whatever small respect she had been preserving for him. Not in a dramatic moment — just quietly, finally, the way a thread gives out after being stretched too long. For literally not having any consideration for her feelings. In a function of all places.

She finished her food.

She looked around.

"Oh — I'm coming." To no one in particular. She turned back to him with the smile. "Ok uncle, I'll talk with you again. Have to go."

"Ok Sia. Keep in mind all the things I said. See ya."

She nodded. Smiled. Turned.

The smile faded before she had taken three steps.

She kept her plate at the dustbin. Found a tap. Washed her hands.

And stood there — hands on the edge of the sink, water still running, the noise of the function carrying on behind her completely unaware.

She breathed in.

Out.

In.

Out.

The specific exhaustion of having held something in for a long time settled over her — not collapse, just weight. The particular feeling of having just cleared something that shouldn't have been in the way to begin with.

Her mind went to Horimiya. To Hori and Miyamura and the quiet simplicity of two people who saw each other without all of this in between.

Why is life so complicated, she thought. It can be so simple. Why isn't it possible.

She let out a long breath.

"Haaa."

It seemed her parents were done too. Time to go home.

She straightened up. Dried her hands. Fixed her expression into something that would carry her through the goodbyes.

And walked back out.
