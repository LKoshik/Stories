The alarm didn't stand a chance.

She had set it out of habit — the same time as always, the body's muscle memory overriding the brain's weekend logic — and when it went off she silenced it with the efficiency of someone who had absolutely no intention of actually getting up.

Then she lay there.

The ceiling. The blanket. The particular quality of Saturday morning light coming through the window that was different from every other morning's light in a way she couldn't explain but could feel.

Her body said — stay.

She stayed.

She rolled into her blanket, pulling it around herself in the specific way of someone constructing an argument against consciousness, and closed her eyes.

— — —

She woke up again twenty minutes later. Then thirty minutes after that. Then once more, briefly, before finally and reluctantly accepting that sleep was done with her even if she wasn't done with it.

She sat up.

Her hair was doing several things at once, none of them coordinated. Her eyes were the squashed kind, still negotiating with the light, lids heavy and uncooperative. She looked at the wall across from her bed — the one with the corkboard and the sticky notes and her mother's reminder she hadn't thrown away — and looked at it with the focused attention of someone who was not actually seeing anything at all.

She was starting her engine.

It was, as always, a process. The kind of process that reminded her of her neighbour's old motorbike back home — the one that needed three or four attempts before it caught, coughing and sputtering, before finally turning over into something resembling function. Her engine was like that. Especially on Saturdays. Especially when there was nowhere to be.

She sat on the edge of the bed for a while.

Then her eyes drifted to the table.

Volume two sat exactly where she'd left it — careful, upright, waiting with the patience of something that knew it would be reached for eventually.

The engine turned over a little faster.

— — —

She went to the bathroom first. Responsible. Measured.

She came back with her face washed and her teeth brushed and her hair bundled inside a towel that sat on the back of her head in a soft uneven hump, the weight of it pulling slightly as she moved. She was wearing the oversized t-shirt she'd slept in — something written across the front in letters that had faded enough to be more suggestion than statement — and her long night pants, and she looked exactly like someone who had made peace with Saturday morning on Saturday morning's terms.

She picked up volume two.

She looked at the sofa chair in the corner — small, slightly sunken from years of use, the kind of chair that had given up on posture but excelled at comfort.

She went to it and fell into it the way water finds the lowest point. Legs taking up the seat beside her, back against one armrest, book open.

She started reading.

— — —

The sofa chair, it turned out, was not designed for the kind of reading she was doing.

She started upright — responsible, both feet on the floor, book held at a reasonable angle. Then her legs migrated to the armrest on the right, dangling over the side. Then her back found the opposite armrest and she was horizontal, book in the air above her face, ceiling and story competing.

The story won.

She kept reading.

Her back started suggesting, politely at first and then less politely, that this was not a sustainable position. She shifted. Readjusted. Found a new configuration that worked for approximately four minutes before something else began to protest.

She shifted again.

This continued.

Somewhere in the middle of a particularly good scene her stomach made its opinion known — a low, patient, entirely reasonable sound that she ignored for one page and then another before finally acknowledging that it had a point.

She looked up from the book.

Fine, she thought.

She got up from the sofa with the energy of someone being asked to do something deeply inconvenient, book still in one hand, other hand on her hip. She walked to the kitchen in this configuration — slowly, reluctantly, the walk of someone whose body was moving toward food while their mind stayed behind on the page.

She stood in front of the stove.

She was still reading.

The lighter was in her hand at some point — she wasn't entirely sure when she'd picked it up — and she stood there holding it with no immediate plans to use it, leaning against the kitchen counter, the book propped against the backsplash.

She read one more page.

Then she looked at herself — lighter in hand, hair towel hump listing slightly to one side, yesterday's oversized t-shirt, the faded writing across her chest — and the reality of the situation arrived with gentle clarity.

She put the lighter down.

I have a perfect idea, she thought.

She filled a bowl with water and put it on the stove.

Cup noodles. Obviously. The answer had been cup noodles this whole time and she had been standing here with a lighter like she was going to do something complicated about it.

She waited for the water to boil with the book back in her hand, giggling softly at something on the page, one eye occasionally checking the bowl, the other refusing to leave the story. When the water was ready she made the noodles with the efficiency of long practice and took them back to the sofa.

She ate slurping, book in the other hand, completely unbothered.

When she finished she took the cup to the sink. Didn't throw it — something in the back of her head, some deeply embedded instinct about objects that still had shape and therefore still had use, made throwing it feel slightly wrong. She put it in the sink and told herself she'd deal with it later.

She went back to the sofa.

— — —

She was halfway through when she stopped.

Not because the story slowed — it hadn't. If anything it had gotten better, tighter, the feeling between the two characters pulled so taut she could feel it through the page. She stopped because she was too full of it. Because reading wasn't enough anymore and she needed to do something with all the feeling that had built up with nowhere to go.

She closed the book carefully. Set it on the table.

She sat on the sofa with one foot on the floor and one knee pulled up to her chest, arms wrapped around her shin, chin resting on her knee.

And she thought.

Specifically — she imagined.

Herself, but different. Cooler. The kind of person who walked into a room and the temperature changed. A tomboy with an effortless energy that people couldn't quite explain but couldn't look away from. She imagined a classroom — girls noticing her, boys noticing her, that particular charged attention of a room that has collectively decided someone is worth watching.

She imagined stepping forward as a car came too fast around a corner, pulling someone out of the way, the whole thing smooth and instinctive and slightly cinematic. She imagined turning to the person she'd saved and saying something — a line, cool and unhurried, the kind of thing that people remembered later and told other people about.

It was an extremely good line.

She was going to regret imagining it later.

She imagined the slow turn, the eye contact held just a beat too long, watching someone across the room go slightly pink and short circuit in the particular way of someone who has been looked at by exactly the right person at exactly the right moment.

She got up from the sofa.

She was enacting it now — this happened sometimes, the imagination overflowing its container — moving through her small apartment with the unhurried swagger of someone who had never stubbed a toe in their life, never shown up to work flustered, never checked a door handle three times.

The slow walk. The look over the shoulder. The line, delivered to the wall with full commitment.

She was doing well. She was doing very well actually. The wall was practically steaming.

She turned—

AUCH—

She grabbed her foot immediately, hopping once before dropping onto the bed, pressing both hands around her little toe with the focused urgency of someone performing emergency first aid on themselves.

Auch. Auch. Auch.

The table sat where it had always sat, entirely unrepentant.

She pressed her toe harder and breathed through her teeth and waited for the specific sharp pain of a stubbed little toe to do what it always eventually did — peak, and then begin, slowly, to recede.

It receded.

She lay back.

Just like that — back on the bed, feet still on the floor at the edge, arms out, staring at the ceiling. The swagger gone. The fantasy dissolved. The wall had moved on.

Silence.

The apartment settled around her. The city outside doing its Saturday thing, indifferent and unhurried. Somewhere below a door closed. Somewhere further a child was making a sound about something.

She lay there and felt the quiet.

Then she got up, gathered some clothes, and went to take a bath.

No particular plan. No particular destination. Just — next.
